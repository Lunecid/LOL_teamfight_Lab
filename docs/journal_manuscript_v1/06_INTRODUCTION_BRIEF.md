## Backward reasoning (answered first)

1. **Technical problem.** Pre-fight information in a MOBA is public, but the earlier target (which team wins the engagement) is local: it says nothing about whether the fight moved the match. A target linked to the match outcome requires a state evaluator that is fixed before prediction, and a test that the predictor adds anything beyond the two things everyone already knows before a fight: the current win probability and the game clock.
2. **Contributions.** (a) A match-linked measurement object: the direction of change in estimated win probability across the engagement interval under a frozen evaluator; (b) a verification stack that separates evaluator quality from direction predictability, tests the lift against flexible functions of initial edge and time, and reports it for two engagement cohorts (teamfights, skirmishes) side by side; (c) bounds on what the label measures (score decomposition, quiet-interval contrast, next-objective correspondence) and on where the lift travels (external score-only checks).
3. **Why it works.** The evaluator is trained on match outcomes and never refit for the prediction task, so the label is fixed before any predictor sees it; contrasts are paired on identical rows with match-cluster bootstrap intervals; hyperparameters and calibrators are chosen on a separate patch and frozen before the test patch is scored.
4. **How prior work leads here.** Encounter/teamfight detection (Schubert et al.; Ke et al.; Tot et al.) defines the unit; win-probability models (Maymin; Jalovaara; Hodge et al.) define the currency; proper-score decomposition (Gneiting–Raftery; Dimitriadis et al.) and direction-of-change forecasting (Christoffersen–Diebold) define the evaluation object. What none of them supplies is the combination: a fight-level direction label under a frozen match evaluator, tested beyond p and t.

## Draft paragraphs (English; author confirmation required)

**P1 — task and application.** Team fights decide most League of Legends matches, and the people watching them, spectators, coaches and broadcast tools, would like to know before a fight starts how much of its consequence is already visible. Esports analytics has built encounter detectors and win-probability displays for exactly this audience [schubert2016encounter; block2018narrative; kokkinakis2020dax; pedrassoli2024passive]. A win-probability number shown on a broadcast, however, changes during a fight for reasons the display cannot explain, and commentators are left to improvise [pedrassoli2024wincondition]. The question this article asks is narrower and testable: given only public state before a fight, can a model say which way the match win probability will move?

**P2 — prior target and its limit.** Prior fight-level prediction targets the winner of the engagement [ke2022; tot2021camera; katona2019time], and match-level prediction targets the winner of the match [costa2021; hitar2023; hodge2021win; silva2018continuous]. Neither target says whether a particular fight moved the match. A fight can be "won" on kills and lose map control, or traded evenly and still shift the game because of what it made possible next. The conference version of this work predicted the gold winner of an engagement from pre-fight state [CoG ref, third person; \pending{anonymity}]; that label is local to the fight and does not connect to the match outcome. \pending{cog-ref}

**P3 — the measurement move.** We define the outcome of a fight as the direction of change in estimated match win probability across the engagement interval, measured by a frozen evaluator trained on match outcomes. Valuing in-game events by the win probability they add has precedent in League of Legends, where "smart kills" and "worthless deaths" were defined by a win-probability model [maymin2021smart], and in learned win-probability estimators used for item valuation [jalovaara2024win]. We extend that currency from single events to the fight interval, with two constraints that make it a measurement rather than a moving target: the evaluator is fit once on match outcomes and frozen, and the direction label is computed before any predictor is trained. Whether the evaluator is well calibrated is checked separately [kim2020confidence; DimitriadisGneitingJordan2021].

**P4 — the question and the challenge.** The question is not whether fight direction can be predicted at all, but whether pre-fight state predicts it beyond the two quantities known before every fight: the current win probability and the game clock. Direction of change and mean change are different objects, and a series can have a predictable sign with an unpredictable mean or the reverse [ChristoffersenDiebold2006]. Answering the question therefore needs baselines that are flexible functions of win probability and time, proper scores compared on identical rows [GneitingRaftery2007], intervals that respect that engagements from one match are not independent [CameronMiller2015], and a protocol in which every choice is made on a patch other than the one used for evaluation.

**P5 — pipeline (Figure 1).** Figure 1 shows the chain. Engagements are detected on 210 000 matches from patches 15.14 to 15.16 with a definition whose constants are estimated from the data, and grouped by the smaller side's participation into teamfights (at least four champions) and skirmishes (two or three). A frozen multilayer-perceptron evaluator scores the state fifteen seconds before the first kill and the state at an endpoint anchored at the last kill; the sign of the difference is the label. A regularized logistic model on 351 pre-fight features plus the pre-fight win probability is compared with a spline baseline in win probability and time. Training uses patch 15.14 with out-of-fold evaluators, selection uses patch 15.15, and evaluation uses patch 15.16; later patches and another region are scored without refitting.

**P6 — results.** Within each cohort the pre-fight model lowers the match-weighted Brier score relative to the spline baseline by about 0.0037 for teamfights (32 981 engagements) and 0.0034 for skirmishes (101 205), with match-cluster bootstrap intervals that exclude zero. The teamfight model applied unchanged to skirmishes keeps part of that lift, and pooling the two cohorts into one training set helps neither. In balanced states the lift is smaller. On patch 16.13 in Korea and North America the teamfight lift does not hold under score-only transfer, while score decomposition shows the discrimination component still favouring the model. The cohorts are reported side by side and are not ranked against each other.

**P7 — contributions.** This article contributes: (1) a match-linked, evaluator-defined direction target for engagements, with the evaluator frozen before prediction; (2) a verification stack that separates evaluator quality from direction predictability, tests the lift against flexible functions of initial win probability and time on two cohorts, and reports transfer and pooling contrasts on identical rows; (3) bounds on what the label measures (quiet-interval contrast, material and next-objective correspondence, horizon sensitivity) and on where the lift travels. We do not claim that one engagement scale is easier to predict than the other, we offer no mechanism for their difference, and we do not present the logistic model as the best available architecture.
