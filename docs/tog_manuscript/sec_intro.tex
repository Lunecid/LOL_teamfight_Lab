\IEEEPARstart{I}{n} League of Legends (LoL), two teams of five players, each controlling one
champion, compete to destroy the opposing base (Section~\ref{sec:background}). Schubert et al.\
made fights a unit of analysis in such games by segmenting Dota~2 matches into spatio-temporal
encounters and predicting win probabilities from them~\cite{schubert2016encounter}. Match data also
feed audience tools: broadcast graphics~\cite{block2018narrative}, a companion
application~\cite{kokkinakis2020dax}, an interactive streaming
extension~\cite{pedrassoli2024passive}, spectator dashboards~\cite{charleer2018dashboards} and live
win prediction~\cite{hodge2021win}. Spectator dashboards must manage viewers' cognitive load and
earn trust through transparent design~\cite{charleer2018dashboards}, and black-box win predictions
leave commentators to improvise interpretations~\cite{pedrassoli2024wincondition}. A transparent
readout attached to a fight before it starts would state how likely each side is to win it, how
much the match depends on it, and how much of that likelihood the scoreboard alone implies;
analysts could use it to find the fights that went against the pre-fight state.

The conference version of this study defined an \emph{engagement} as a cluster of champion kills
in Riot's public Match-V5 records~\cite{riotapi2024matchv5,riotapi2024timeline} and predicted its
winner from the 30\,s before a cutoff 10\,s before the first kill, reaching an area under the
receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.675 on a held-out patch (the AUC is the
probability that a model scores a random engagement won by blue above one lost by blue; 0.5 is
chance). Reviewers questioned its thresholds (18\,s between kills, 4,000 map units across a
cluster), label weights, deep baselines, observation window and kill-based scope. We therefore ask
two questions. Is the number a property of our definition and label, or of what the telemetry
records before a fight? And what bounds it: the learner, the label, the window, or the information
available at the cutoff?

Answering the first question required checking every input against the moment of prediction, and
the audit found two inputs that used the future: game time divided by the length of the whole
match, and map anchors that included structures and objectives taken later. On these inputs, our
analyses after the conference version had concluded that pre-fight predictability decomposes by
scale. Engagements were classed by the number of participants on the smaller side as picks (at most
one), skirmishes and teamfights, and teamfights, whether the class began at three participants or at
four, were markedly more predictable than the rest. We withdraw that conclusion. In a paired ablation
on 5,000 matches, the time feature alone raised the AUC of teamfights by 0.049 and that of picks by
0.003. With audit-fixed inputs, LightGBM gradient-boosted trees~\cite{ke2017lightgbm}, many small
decision trees added one after another, each fitted to the errors of those before it, reach an
AUC of 0.6699 on 532,547 engagements from 191,940 matches in five-fold cross-validation that keeps
each match within one fold, and the difference between the AUCs of picks and teamfights follows
the teamfight cut, a convention: $+0.0108$ with the cut at three participants, $-0.0019$ at four and
$-0.0105$ at five. We therefore make no claim about scale in either direction
(Section~\ref{sec:prediction}). The conference
pipeline used both inputs, so its 0.675 is not a clean reference. We count the withdrawal as a
finding of the audit: dividing by match length is an easy step when features are built from
complete timelines.

\item \emph{A negative result for tool builders, and where the value lies.} Adding an engagement
  prediction computed from a match model's own pre-fight inputs did not improve that model. A
  gradient-boosted model of the match winner given the engagement's pre-fight columns reaches an AUC
  of 0.8124 on 26,712 held-out engagements; adding a prediction of the direction in which the
  engagement moves win probability (held-out AUC 0.583) gives 0.8125 (95\% interval of the difference
  [$-0.00055$, $+0.00082$]). A synthetic predictor of that direction with AUC 0.669, built from the
  realised direction plus noise and so informed from outside the inputs, does raise the AUC
  ([$+0.0008$, $+0.0028$]). Our engagement-outcome predictor (held-out AUC 0.665 on its own label), which
  is also computed from the match model's inputs, gives 0.8125 as well ([$-0.0010$, $+0.0013$]).
  Supplying the realised
  direction only for the quarter of engagements with the largest change in win probability raises the
  AUC by 0.0196 [0.0170, 0.0224], and only for the quarter with the smallest change by 0.0001. The
  quarters are defined by the realised change, so this holds partly by construction, but it locates
  where better pre-fight information would pay (Section~\ref{sec:limit}).

Several analyses that the reviews asked for have finished. In the label family, the adopted label and
eleven variants, scored on the same engagements, reach five-fold AUCs between 0.6737 and 0.6884
(Section~\fwdref{sec:label-family}). The stacking of our engagement-outcome predictor into the match
model is reported above. Reconstructing the cutoff state from the event log on the full corpus raises
the held-out-patch AUC from 0.6665 to 0.6732, by $+0.0068$ [$+0.0057$, $+0.0078$], of which
$+0.0051$ [$+0.0041$, $+0.0060$] is gained over the same columns filled from the held frame
(Section~\fwdref{sec:limit-evidence}). In the observation-window sweep, on 50,766 engagements from a
20,000-match sample, finer bins (2.5\,s instead of 5\,s within the 30\,s window) raise the
held-out-patch AUC of LightGBM by $+0.0063$ [$+0.0029$, $+0.0100$] (Holm-adjusted bootstrap
$p \le 0.005$), the only change whose interval stays above zero after adjustment for the five
comparisons; windows of 60\,s and 120\,s change it by $+0.0038$ and $+0.0042$, neither above zero after
that adjustment. Two sequence learners that read the bins in order, a BiGRU and a temporal Transformer,
untuned and trained once, stay below LightGBM (0.616 and 0.627 against 0.638 with the 30\,s window)
and gain nothing from longer windows (Section~\ref{sec:learners-window}). In the attribution check for
leaks, a model given both leaking inputs scores $+0.0165$ [$+0.0122$, $+0.0211$] AUC above the
audit-fixed model on 20,000 sampled matches, and it places 9.2\% of its attribution (SHAP values, which
split each prediction into contributions of the input columns~\cite{lundberg2020local}) on the
leak-affected columns, against 0.5\% for the audit-fixed model; almost all of it falls on game time
divided by match length (Section~\ref{sec:limit-shap}). Re-detecting the engagements of a
10,000-match sample with the kill gap between 10 and 18\,s and the diameter between 3,500 and
4,750\,u changes their number by $-8.5$\% to $+11.6$\% and, on the engagements found both there and at
the adopted constants, the held-out-patch AUC by $-0.0094$ to $+0.0036$. On all 210,000 cached matches,
moving the presence gate to the conference version's values nearly doubles the number of engagements and
raises the held-out-patch AUC by $+0.0071$ [$+0.0050$, $+0.0094$]. On the engagements that both gates
accept the rise is $+0.0039$ [$+0.0025$, $+0.0053$], and the run does not separate how much of it comes
from the gate's later cutoff and how much from its larger training set. Requiring three champions per side
instead of two leaves 13.5\% as many engagements as the adopted gate and raises the AUC by $+0.0124$
[$+0.0057$, $+0.0191$], through which engagements remain rather than through a better model. The result
is thus not invariant to the presence gate
(Section~\ref{sec:pred-constants}).
