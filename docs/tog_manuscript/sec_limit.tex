\section{Where the Limit Comes From}\label{sec:limit}

Section~\fwdref{sec:prediction} ranks the outcomes of engagements before they begin with an AUC of
about 0.67, and Section~\ref{sec:learners} asks whether another learner ranks them better. This section
asks two further questions: what an engagement's outcome is worth to the match, and whether the limit on
predicting it lies in the game or in what the telemetry records at the cutoff. We first build a model of
the probability that blue wins the match (Section~\ref{sec:limit-value}) and take its change over an
engagement as the engagement's value (Section~\ref{sec:limit-delta}). We then measure how much of that
value the outcome accounts for (Section~\ref{sec:limit-explained}), whether a predicted outcome improves
a prediction of the match (Section~\ref{sec:limit-stacking}), and why a calibrated probability leaves
little of an engagement's value to predict (Section~\ref{sec:limit-martingale}). Finally, we test whether
part of the limit is observational by reconstructing the state at the cutoff from the event log
(Section~\ref{sec:limit-evidence}), and describe how attributions are used to check that no leaked input
carries a result (Section~\ref{sec:limit-shap}).
% 0.67: FEAT/scale_decomposition_v33_market_event.json overall_auc 0.6699089924634439 (quoted in sec_learners.tex).

Sections~\ref{sec:limit-value}--\ref{sec:limit-martingale} use a working sample of 49,677 matches from
the same cache, patches and definition as the corpus, selected by a hash of each match identifier and
therefore independently of how the matches ended. Its matches are divided once into disjoint groups:
10,007 fit, calibrate and select the win-probability model, 4,986 test it, 24,840 train engagement
predictors and 9,844 test them. In these sections every AUC, Brier score, calibration error, $R^2$ and
sign accuracy is \emph{match-weighted}: an engagement from a match with $k$ engagements has weight $1/k$,
so every match counts equally and matches with many engagements do not dominate.
Section~\ref{sec:limit-evidence} uses the full labelled corpus and the patch holdout of
Section~\ref{sec:learners} and, like that section, reports unweighted AUCs.

\subsection{A Model of Match Win Probability}\label{sec:limit-value}

Gold measures what a fight yields, not what it does to the match. The gold-priced label, for example,
gives an elemental drake a fitted price of 0 (Section~\fwdref{sec:label}), although a team's fourth drake
grants the Dragon Soul (Table~\ref{tab:terms}), and Section~\ref{sec:limit-martingale} shows that winning
an engagement moves the match by very different amounts depending on how close the match is. A measure
that reads every situation on one scale is the probability that a team wins the match given what is
observable at a moment. Maymin fitted such a model for LoL and
valued kills and deaths by the change in it~\cite{maymin2021smart}; we value engagements the same way.
We write $V(S)$ for the model's probability that blue wins the match given the observable state $S$ at a
time $t$.
% Drake fitted price 0: sec_background.tex Table tab:terms, row "Outcome label" ("a drake's fitted price is 0").
% "very different amounts": tab:limit-swing, M_plus +14.13 at V_pre 0.456-0.512 vs +2.19 above 0.872 (martingale_swing.json).

The state $S$ contains the game time and its square. For each of the ten players it contains the values
of the most recent frame at or before $t$ (total and current gold, level, experience, share of health and
mana, whether alive, lane and jungle creep score), the champion, and the player's kills, deaths and time
since the last death. For each team it contains counts of the events up to $t$ (kills, drakes by element,
plates, towers by type, inhibitors, epic monsters and Dragon Souls) and how long ago the team last took
each epic monster. Products of 94 of these team values with game time let their effect change as the
match goes on. The model reads 361 inputs in all, and nothing after $t$.
% 361 = WT/outputs/state_value_main_50k/schema.json state_names (362) minus snapshot_age_s, which
%   WT/train/temporal_winprob.py feature_matrix() drops for the "expanded" family; 94 names end in "_x_time";
%   10 names end in "champion_id" (one-hot in WT/train/state_value_experiment.py logistic()).
% Frame values vs event counts: WT/gameplay/state_value.py StateBuilder.at(): snapshot index = last minute_ts <= q
%   (searchsorted side right); events = those with timestamp <= q (bisect_right).

Following Maymin~\cite{maymin2021smart}, the model is a logistic regression: it forms a weighted sum of
the inputs and passes it through the logistic function, which maps any number to a probability between 0
and 1. With 361 inputs, the weights can fit accidental regularities of the training matches, so a penalty
on their size is added. Its strength was chosen from four candidates by three-fold cross-validation
grouped by match: the fitting matches are split into three groups, each candidate is fitted on two groups
and scored on the third, in turn, and the candidate with the lowest log loss is kept, where log loss is
the average of $-\log$ of the probability given to what happened. A model can rank states well while its
probabilities are systematically too high or too low, so the probabilities were then recalibrated on
separate matches. Two recalibrations were tried: Platt scaling, a one-input logistic regression on the
model's log-odds~\cite{platt2000probabilities}, and isotonic regression, a stepwise mapping from old to
new probabilities that is constrained only never to decrease~\cite{zadrozny2002transforming}. Platt
scaling gave a lower log loss on a third set of matches than either isotonic regression or no
recalibration. These three sets hold 7,004, 1,501 and 1,502 matches.

The model learns only from final match results attached to states sampled from whole matches; no
engagement is used to train it, and no match of the engagement groups is among its matches. States lie on
a one-minute grid, and consecutive states of a match are strongly correlated, which led Maymin to sample
one random minute per match~\cite{maymin2021smart}. That sample inherits the distribution of match
lengths: sampled that way, only 211 of our 7,004 fitting states (3.0\%) fell after minute 30. We
therefore follow Jalovaara~\cite{jalovaara2024win} and sample one state from each 5-minute interval of
each match, which gives 2,517 of 40,228 fitting states (6.3\%) after minute 30. Nothing else in the
procedure changed, except that the same selection then chose a stronger penalty.

On one random minute of each of the 4,986 test matches, $V$ reaches an AUC of 0.846 and a Brier score of
0.160, where the Brier score is the mean squared difference between the predicted probability and the
result (1 if blue won, 0 otherwise). A seven-input logistic regression on Maymin's inputs (minutes
elapsed and each team's kills, towers and epic monsters), fitted on the same states, reaches 0.828 and
0.171 on the same minutes.

Ranking is not enough for what follows, because an engagement's value is a difference of two
probabilities, and a systematic error in the probabilities passes into the difference. A model is
\emph{calibrated} if, among the states it gives probability $p$, blue wins about a fraction $p$ of the
matches~\cite{naeini2015obtaining}. We summarise calibration by the expected calibration error
(ECE)~\cite{naeini2015obtaining,guo2017calibration}: the predictions are sorted into ten bins of equal
width on $[0, 1]$, the gap between the mean predicted probability and the observed win rate is taken in
each bin, and the gaps are averaged with weights equal to each bin's share of the predictions. An ECE of
0.02 means that predictions miss the observed rate by about two percentage points, averaged over bins. On
the random test minutes, the ECE of $V$ is 0.029.
% ECE: WT/scripts/run_temporal_winprob_v3_buckets.py ece_from() and WT/scripts/validate_temporal_winprob_claude.py reliability():
%   10 equal-width bins on [0, 1], share = match-weight share of the bin, sum of share * |observed - predicted|.
% 0.029: results.json test_one_minute.expanded.ece 0.0287681.

A model calibrated at random minutes need not be calibrated at engagement cutoffs: a cutoff is placed
15\,s before a kill that is known to follow, so the moments are selected. We therefore measured
calibration on the 26,712 engagements of the 9,198 engagement-test matches that have one, at the four
instants defined in Section~\ref{sec:limit-delta}. The ECE is 0.018 at the cutoff, 0.010 at the last kill,
0.023 30\,s later and 0.025 60\,s later, and the AUC for the match result at these instants is 0.805,
0.833, 0.835 and 0.835. By game time at the cutoff, the ECE is 0.009 between minutes 2 and 10, 0.024
between 10 and 20 and 0.012 between 20 and 30. It is worst after minute 30, at 0.069 (1,077 engagements
from 817 matches), where the AUC is 0.844; at the last kill that band's ECE is 0.028. Values measured
after minute 30 therefore rest on the least calibrated probabilities (Section~\ref{subsec:limitations}).

\subsection{The Value of an Engagement}\label{sec:limit-delta}

To state how much an engagement changed the match, we compare $V$ just before it with $V$ just after it:
\begin{equation}
\Delta V = V(S_{\mathrm{end}}) - V(S_{\mathrm{pre}}),
\end{equation}
in percentage points from blue's side, so that $\Delta V = +10$ means blue's probability of winning the
match rose by ten points. The two states follow a fixed time contract.
\begin{itemize}
\item $S_{\mathrm{pre}}$ is the state at $\tau - 1$\,ms, where $\tau$ is the cutoff, 15\,s before the
  engagement's first kill (Table~\ref{tab:terms}). The predictors' inputs end at the same instant.
\item $S_{\mathrm{end}}$ is the state at the timestamp of the engagement's last kill. The telemetry records
  no end of combat, so the last kill stands in for it.
\item The state at time $t$ contains every event with a timestamp at most $t$ and the most recent frame at
  or before $t$. At the last kill, $V$ therefore sees the engagement's kills but not yet the gold and
  experience they paid, which arrive with the next frame.
\item Two later horizons, 30 and 60\,s after the last kill, let the frames catch up and count objectives
  taken just after the fight, at the cost of also counting whatever happened next. A horizon that falls
  after the match's end or its last frame is dropped, which removes 617 and 1,362 of the 26,712
  engagements.
\end{itemize}
$V$ is fitted once, before any engagement is scored, and never sees an engagement.

\subsection{How Much of the Value the Outcome Explains}\label{sec:limit-explained}

If who wins an engagement determines most of its value, then predicting the outcome before the fight
predicts most of what the fight does to the match. If the state before the fight already fixes the
value, the outcome adds little. We compare the two directly. For the 63,457 training and 25,177 test
engagements with a decided \texttt{market\_event} outcome, gradient-boosted regression trees
(LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm}) predict $\Delta V$ from three sets of inputs: the 7,106 pre-cutoff
columns of Section~\ref{sec:learners-input}; the realised outcome alone, a single column that is 1 if blue
won; and both. Two scores are reported. $R^2$ is the share of the variance of $\Delta V$ across test
engagements that the predictions account for: 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than always predicting the
mean, and it can be negative. \emph{Sign accuracy} is the share of test engagements whose predicted and
realised $\Delta V$ have the same sign.

Table~\ref{tab:limit-explained} gives the result. At the last kill, the realised outcome alone explains
55.0\% of the variance of $\Delta V$ and gives its sign for 92.4\% of engagements, which is how often the
side that the gold-priced label names as the winner is the side whose win probability rose. The
pre-cutoff columns alone explain 2.4\% and give the sign for 55.6\%, and both together explain 65.7\%.
What an engagement does to the match is therefore decided mainly by who wins it, not by the state before
it. The outcome's share of the variance falls with game time, from 0.630 between minutes 2 and 10 to
0.361 between 20 and 30, while its sign accuracy does not (0.920 to 0.931), and it falls with the
horizon, to 0.413 after 30\,s and 0.324 after 60\,s, as later events enter the change. The weak $R^2$ of
the pre-cutoff columns does not contradict Section~\fwdref{sec:prediction}: the same columns rank who wins
the engagement with an AUC of 0.665 in this sample, but $\Delta V$ takes the sign of the outcome, and
given the state before the fight its expected value is close to zero
(Section~\ref{sec:limit-martingale}). Adding an out-of-fold predicted outcome to the pre-cutoff columns
changes the pooled $R^2$ at the last kill by $+0.0005$ (95\% interval $-0.0019$ to $+0.0029$).

\subsection{Does a Predicted Outcome Improve Match Prediction?}\label{sec:limit-stacking}

If the outcome carries most of an engagement's value and can be predicted before the engagement, a
natural next step is to give that prediction to a model of the match winner, as a tool that shows live
win probabilities might. Using the output of one model as an input to another is called
\emph{stacking}~\cite{wolpert1992stacked}. So that the second model never learns from predictions made
with knowledge of its own training rows, the added column is computed \emph{out of fold} on the
training matches: the matches are split into five groups, and each group's engagements are predicted by
a model fitted on the other four.

A null is what the construction leads one to expect, whatever the predictor's accuracy. The column added
in B or B$'$ is computed from the same pre-cutoff columns that A reads, so it carries no information that
A's inputs lack: A's inputs are contained in B's, and the one extra column is a function of them, so the
best prediction achievable from B's inputs is the best achievable from A's. A tree ensemble fitted on A's
inputs can in principle represent that function itself. In a finite sample a learner may still gain from
a precomputed summary, so the argument does not forbid a gain, but it explains a null without any
appeal to the game, and a null here says nothing about whether engagements matter to the match.

\subsection{Why a Calibrated Probability Leaves Little to Predict}\label{sec:limit-martingale}

A calibrated win probability behaves like a fair price. Suppose $V$ at the cutoff were exactly the
probability that blue wins given everything $V$ reads. Averaged over all the ways the next seconds can
go, its later value must then equal its present value: a forecaster that expected its own number to rise
would already have raised it. A sequence of forecasts with this property is called a \emph{martingale}.
For engagements it means that, given the state at the cutoff, the expected $\Delta V$ is zero: $V$ has
already priced the fight about to happen. A fight that blue is likely to win therefore cannot show up as
a positive expected change. It shows up as an asymmetry. Let $q$ be the probability that blue wins the
engagement, $M_+$ the mean change when it does and $M_-$ (negative) the mean change when it does not. A
zero mean requires $q M_+ + (1-q) M_- = 0$, that is
\begin{equation}
q = \frac{-M_-}{M_+ - M_-},
\end{equation}
so the favourite gains little by winning and loses much by losing. The argument is exact only for moments
chosen without knowledge of the future; a cutoff is placed before a kill that is known to follow, so small
departures can occur.

rank correlation measures whether two quantities put engagements in the same order: it is the correlation

\subsection{Observation as a Bound: Reconstructing the State at the Cutoff}\label{sec:limit-evidence}

The pre-cutoff columns hold the values of the most recent frame. Frames arrive every 60\,s, and at the
cutoff the most recent one is a median 33.6\,s old (Section~\ref{subsec:limitations}), whereas events
carry millisecond timestamps. Holding a frame is right for a quantity that changes only at events, such
as level, as long as no such event happened after the frame, and wrong for a quantity that accumulates
continuously, such as gold. In 99.6\% of the frame gaps audited below, the match log records at least one
event. If the limit on pre-fight prediction is partly a matter of what the frames show, correcting the
state at the cutoff from these events should raise it; if the limit is a property of the game, it
should not.
% 33.6 s: sec_limitations.tex (FEAT/prediction_situation_pilot.json checks.freshness.age_s_quantiles p50 33.60).
% 99.6%: REV/A4-evidence-state-leak/audit_predict_test_1000_v2curve.json share_with_any_event_in_gap 0.995839 (any event of the
%   match in (frame j-1, tau], WT/scripts/audit_evidence_state.py main(): event_ts over all events of the match).

The reconstruction treats each field according to how it changes, and never reads anything after the
cutoff.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Known exactly from events}: level (from level-up events), ultimate rank, items held (from
  purchase, sale, undo and destruction events), whether a champion is alive and when it respawns (from its
  deaths and the respawn timer), Baron and Elder buffs, and Dragon Souls.
\item \emph{Approximated from events}: champion statistics such as attack damage, armour and maximum
  health, as the frame's value plus the statistics of items bought or sold since and the growth from
  levels gained, read from Riot's Data Dragon tables for versions 15.14.1--15.16.1 (619 items and 171
  champions each). Ability haste, armour and magic penetration and omnivamp are not in those tables and
  are held, and unique item passives are not modelled.
\item \emph{Extrapolated}: total gold, experience and creep score, as the frame's value plus the rate over
  the minute before that frame times the frame's age. Gold also adds the bounties of the champion's kills
  since the frame, current gold also adds sale refunds and subtracts purchases, and experience is kept
  within the range that the level from level-up events allows.
\item \emph{Fused}: position, chosen among the frame's position and positions that events imply (the
  champion's own kills and assists, the epic monsters, structures and plates it destroyed, and its
  fountain after a respawn or purchase) as the candidate with the smallest expected error for its kind
  and age.
\item \emph{Held}: health and mana between events (set to zero at a death and to full at a respawn),
  damage totals and crowd-control time.
\end{itemize}
Nothing is interpolated towards the next frame: that frame lies after the cutoff, often after the first
kill, and interpolating towards it would carry the kill into the inputs.

\subsection{Attributions as Leak Forensics}\label{sec:limit-shap}

An input that carries information not available at the cutoff can raise an AUC for the wrong reason, and
the AUC alone does not reveal it. The feature path of the conference submission had two such inputs:
game time was divided by the match's final length, which is unknown at the cutoff, and map anchors were
computed from structures destroyed at any time in the match, including after the cutoff. The corrected
path divides game time by 45 minutes, capped at one, and keeps only structures destroyed before the cutoff
(Section~\ref{sec:def-constants}). On 12,603 engagements from 5,000 matches, with match-grouped five-fold
out-of-fold LightGBM, restoring both leaks raises the AUC from 0.620 to 0.640, almost all of it through
game time (0.639 with that leak alone, 0.624 with the anchor leak alone). An AUC shows that a leak helps;
it does not show where in the inputs a model finds it.

%   fact 1); leaky definitions: REPO/scripts/run_shap_leak_forensics_v33.py docstring (time_norm = t / (match length - 1);
%   anchors = every tower / objective destroyed anywhere in the match).  Corrected path: sec_definition.tex sec:def-constants
%   (game time divided by 45 minutes and capped at one; anchors before the cutoff).
% FEAT/leak_ablation_v33.json n_matches 5000, configs.clean.n_rows 12603, auc 0.619726; time_leak 0.639154; anchor_leak 0.624101;
%   both_leaks 0.639965.  Protocol: REPO/scripts/run_leak_ablation.py docstring (same fight refs, flags toggled, match-grouped

Attribution is the tool for that question. For each prediction, SHAP values split the difference between
the model's output and its average output into one additive contribution per input column, using the
Shapley value from cooperative game theory, and TreeSHAP computes them exactly for tree
ensembles~\cite{lundberg2020local}. We use them for one purpose. The same engagements are built twice,
with the corrected inputs and with both leaks; the columns a leak affects are found by rebuilding the
inputs with each switch, not listed by hand; one LightGBM model per build is trained under the patch
holdout; and on the same test engagements we compare the share of mean absolute SHAP value that falls on
the leak columns, with match-clustered intervals, together with the paired AUC difference. If the leaky
model's extra accuracy comes through the leaks, that model should concentrate its attribution on the leak
columns and the model trained without them should not. The SHAP values are also
checked against the Shapley definition by code that shares nothing with LightGBM.

The run uses 54,561 engagements from a random sample of 20,000 matches; 15,911 engagements from 5,695
matches form the test patch. The model with both leaks reaches a test AUC of 0.6637 against 0.6471
without them, a paired difference of 0.0165 (95\% interval 0.0122 to 0.0211, from 2,000 bootstrap
resamples of the test matches). On 10,000 test engagements, the leaky model places 9.2\% of its mean
absolute SHAP value on the leak columns and the audit-fixed model 0.5\%, a paired difference of 8.7
percentage points (8.6 to 8.9). Almost all of it is game time: in the leaky model the time quantity
holds 9.0\% of the attribution and ranks second among the base quantities, behind the gold difference,
whereas in the audit-fixed model, where it carries elapsed minutes divided by 45, it holds 0.1\% and
ranks 154th. The anchor columns hold 0.2\% in the leaky model and 0.3\% in the audit-fixed one, in line
with the small AUC effect of the anchor leak above. On every checked tree and engagement the SHAP values
agree with the Shapley definition to within $10^{-15}$. We do not
reproduce the attribution ranking of the conference submission: it was computed on the leaky feature path,
where a ranking cannot separate the game's structure from the leaks.

\subsection{Summary}\label{sec:limit-summary}

Who wins an engagement decides most of what it does to the match: at the last kill the outcome alone
explains 55\% of the variance of the change in win probability, and the state before the engagement
explains 2\%. A prediction of that outcome made from the same pre-cutoff columns does not improve a match
model that already reads those columns, as nested inputs lead one to expect, so this null says nothing
about whether engagements matter to the match. A synthetic column informed from outside those columns
helps once its AUC for the direction of the change reaches 0.669, above the 0.583 and 0.622 of the two
real predictors on that label, so the sweep does not separate information source from accuracy. A
calibrated win probability has
already priced the favourite, so the predictability of an engagement appears as asymmetric stakes rather
than as an expected gain. Finally, reconstructing the state at the cutoff from events that 60\,s frames
