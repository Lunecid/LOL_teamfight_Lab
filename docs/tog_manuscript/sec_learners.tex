\section{Is the Ceiling a Model-Class Limit?}
\label{sec:learners}

% 0.6699: REV/A6-definition-sensitivity/scale_cut_sensitivity_v33.json overall.auc.point 0.669909 (= FEAT/
%   scale_decomposition_v33_market_event.json overall_auc 0.6699089924634439).  0.6665: A8 learners.lgbm_paper.
%   test_auc 0.6664835319909197.
Section~\ref{sec:prediction} ranks the outcomes of engagements before they begin with gradient-boosted
trees, at an AUC of 0.6699 under grouped cross-validation and 0.6665 on a held-out patch. These values
describe one learner. By themselves they cannot say whether the information available before an
engagement allows no better ranking, or whether a more flexible learner would find structure that the
trees miss. We call the highest AUC that this information allows the \emph{ceiling}.
Section~\ref{sec:limit} attributes part of the limit to what the telemetry records before an engagement;
that reading presumes that a different learner would not rank the same engagements much better from the
same inputs. This section therefore asks one question: \emph{given identical inputs, does any learner rank
engagement outcomes better than gradient-boosted trees?} It also tests a second explanation raised in
review: that the 30\,s observation window is too short or too coarse, above all for learners that read
time in order (Section~\ref{sec:learners-window}).

The answer to the first question is not complete. The comparison first run for this study gave the
neural learners no hyper-parameter search and sizes that differ by a factor of about fifteen, and two of its
rows are withdrawn because of implementation errors (Section~\ref{sec:learners-published}). Three
corrected or enlarged runs have finished; two more runs and a search declared in advance are running or
queued (Section~\ref{sec:learners-effort}). Their cells in Table~\ref{tab:learners} are marked as pending, and no
conclusion about model classes is drawn until they are filled.

\subsection{Terms}
\label{sec:learners-terms}

A \emph{learner} is a family of prediction functions together with a procedure that fits one member of
the family to labelled examples. The values that fitting sets, such as the weights of a neural network,
are the model's \emph{trainable parameters}; settings fixed before fitting, such as the number of trees
or the learning rate, are \emph{hyper-parameters}. Engagements are divided into a \emph{training} set,
used to fit parameters, a \emph{validation} set, used to choose hyper-parameters and to decide when to
stop training, and a \emph{test} set, scored once at the end. A neural network is trained in
\emph{epochs}, full passes over the training set. A learner that fits accidental regularities of its
training set, and therefore predicts new engagements worse, \emph{overfits}. AUC, early stopping and the
match bootstrap are defined in Section~\ref{sec:pred-protocol}.

\subsection{The Ladder of Learners}
\label{sec:learners-ladder}

Whether trees or neural networks should be expected to rank better on a table like ours is open
(Section~\ref{sec:related}). The papers that introduced neural architectures for tables report gains on
their own benchmarks~\cite{arik2021tabnet,somepalli2021saint}. Independent comparisons found
gradient-boosted trees ahead on most datasets~\cite{shwartzziv2022tabular,grinsztajn2022tree}, and under
one tuning protocol neither family was superior on every dataset~\cite{gorishniy2021revisiting}.
Findings on other data do not settle the question for ours, so we measure it. The learners form a ladder,
ordered roughly by how much structure each can represent. Each rung is there because its result, in
either direction, says something specific about the ceiling.

\paragraph{Scoreboard model}
This model is LightGBM in its published configuration (below), given eight columns: the blue-minus-red
differences in gold, experience, average champion level, kills, creep score and living champions at the
last bin, game time, and frame age. It measures how far the rule ``the team that is ahead wins'' carries.
The distance from it to the full-input learners is what the rest of the input contributes, and a learner
that falls below it ranks engagements worse than the lead in these eight columns does.

\paragraph{Logistic regression}
The model forms a weighted sum of the 6,164 informative columns, each standardised, and passes it
through the logistic function to obtain a probability. The weights are fitted by gradient descent on the
cross-entropy between predictions and labels with weight decay~\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled}, which
shrinks every weight toward zero at each step so that thousands of weights do not fit noise, and
training stops early on the validation patch. It is the least flexible learner that uses every
informative column: it cannot represent a threshold, nor an interaction such as a gold lead that matters
more late in a match than early. What the trees add to it is what thresholds and interactions add.

\paragraph{Gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM)}
A decision tree predicts by asking a short sequence of threshold questions about single columns, such as
whether the gold difference exceeds some value. Gradient boosting builds many small trees one after
another, fits each new tree to the errors that the trees before it still make, and adds their outputs;
LightGBM is an efficient implementation~\cite{ke2017lightgbm}. Early stopping watches the score on the
validation patch while trees are added, ends training once that score has not improved for a fixed number
of trees, and keeps the model at its best point, because further trees fit accidental regularities of the
training patch. The \emph{published configuration} (at most 400 trees of at most 31 leaves, learning rate
0.05, 90\% of the columns drawn for each tree) is the learner of Section~\ref{sec:prediction}. It uses
early stopping with a patience of 100 trees; under the patch holdout its best iteration is 381 of at most
400, so training ran to the cap and the model at tree 381 is kept. A \emph{higher-capacity configuration}
(at most 3,000 trees of at most 127 leaves, learning rate 0.02) asks whether the published trees are simply
too small, and the declared search (Section~\ref{sec:learners-effort}) gives LightGBM the same search as
every other learner.

\paragraph{Multilayer perceptron (MLP)}
An MLP passes the input through a stack of layers; each layer forms weighted sums of all outputs of the
layer below and applies a fixed non-linear function, so the stack can combine many columns at once. It
reads the same 7,106 columns, each standardised with the mean and standard deviation of the training
patch. It enters as the baseline neural learner on identical inputs, the role it has in the benchmark of
Gorishniy et al.~\cite{gorishniy2021revisiting}: the simplest network, which a more elaborate
architecture must at least match. The conference version called a similar network a ``diagnostic'' that
separated the effect of the engineered input from that of the tree learner, without a reference. We drop
that framing, because two learners compared on one input separate learners, not inputs. For the same
reason, attention or convolutional probes are not the relevant comparison: a probe is a small model
trained to read what a fixed, already learned representation encodes, and here every learner is trained
end to end on the same columns, so there is no fixed representation to probe. Attention therefore enters
as complete learners, FT-Transformer and SAINT below and a temporal Transformer in
Section~\ref{sec:learners-window}. Convolution assumes that neighbouring inputs are related, which holds
for the ordered bins of the window but not for the columns of the table, whose order carries no meaning;
a convolutional learner over the bins was not run.

\paragraph{FT-Transformer}
FT-Transformer~\cite{gorishniy2021revisiting} turns each input quantity into a short vector, a
\emph{token}, by a linear map of its own. It then applies \emph{self-attention}
layers~\cite{vaswani2017attention}: each token is updated with a weighted average of all tokens, whose
weights are computed from the tokens themselves, so which quantities inform one another can differ from
one engagement to the next. A learned summary token feeds the prediction. A tree combines columns through
nested threshold questions on one column at a time; attention can combine many quantities in one step.
FT-Transformer is therefore the most direct test of whether the ceiling reflects interactions that trees
represent poorly, and it is one of the neural models that, under a common tuning protocol, was not
uniformly beaten by gradient-boosted trees~\cite{gorishniy2021revisiting}.

% 60 s frames and "changes at most once": sec_prediction.tex sec:pred-task (frames every ~60 s; a quantity
%   carried forward from the last frame changes at most once inside the 30 s window).
\paragraph{TabNet}
TabNet~\cite{arik2021tabnet} predicts in several decision steps. At each step an attention module selects
a sparse subset of the columns through sparsemax~\cite{martins2016sparsemax}, which gives most columns a
weight of exactly zero, and a prior discourages later steps from reusing columns chosen earlier. The
entropy of the selection masks is added to the training loss, so that training favours masks that select
few columns. TabNet is on the ladder because our input is wide and redundant: several of the seven
statistics of a frame quantity nearly coincide, since the quantity changes at most once inside the
window (Section~\ref{sec:pred-task}). If the other learners were hampered by having to find a few
informative columns among thousands, a learner built to select them should do better.

\paragraph{SAINT}
SAINT~\cite{somepalli2021saint} alternates attention across the quantities of one engagement, as in
FT-Transformer, with attention across the engagements of a batch, so that the representation of one
engagement can draw on others. It adds \emph{self-supervised pre-training}, which uses the inputs but not
the labels. The network learns to give an engagement and a corrupted copy of it (some columns replaced by
those of another engagement~\cite{yun2019cutmix}, and its token vectors mixed with another's~\cite{zhang2018mixup})
similar representations that differ from those of other engagements, a \emph{contrastive}
objective~\cite{chen2020simple}, and to reconstruct the original values from the copy. Attention across
engagements tests whether an engagement is predicted better in the company of others; pre-training tests
whether the labelled training engagements are too few to learn a good representation. We pre-train on
training-patch engagements only, so no feature of the validation or test patch enters it. SAINT is
available as a preprint, not a peer-reviewed publication; it is included because it combines both ideas
in one architecture. Attention across engagements needs care with our data, because an earlier engagement
that shares a batch with a later one of the same match could attend to features recorded after its own
cutoff (Section~\ref{sec:learners-published}).

\paragraph{Sequence learners}
The seven statistics decide, before any learning, which aspects of the history inside the window a
tabular learner can see. A sequence learner instead receives the value of every quantity in every bin and
learns its own summary. The bidirectional gated recurrent unit (BiGRU) reads the bins in order while
carrying a state of fixed size, with learned gates deciding how much of the state each new bin
overwrites~\cite{cho2014learning}; a second reader runs from the last bin to the first. The temporal
Transformer applies self-attention across bins, with an encoding of each bin's
position~\cite{vaswani2017attention}. Recurrent networks have been used for continuous outcome prediction
in League of Legends~\cite{silva2018continuous}, and deep networks for death prediction in
Dota~2~\cite{katona2019time}. These are the learners for which the length and resolution of the window
should matter most, so they are evaluated in the window experiment (Section~\ref{sec:learners-window}).

The conference version compared pairings of an input representation with a learner family: LightGBM and
an MLP on the tabular input, a BiGRU and a Transformer on a sequence of match-level state, graph neural
networks on a graph of the ten players, an attention model relating events to player states, and a
``Layered'' fusion network. The fusion network encoded the sequence, the player graph and the events in
three branches, concatenated the three encodings into one vector $h$, and multiplied a transformed copy of
$h$ element by element with a learned gate $g = \sigma(W h + b)$, where $\sigma$ is the logistic function
applied to each element. ``Layered+Logit'' was the same network with one more input to its prediction
head: the logit $\log\bigl(p/(1-p)\bigr)$ of the probability $p$ predicted by a separately trained
LightGBM model. Neither is part of this study, for three reasons. First, the pairings changed the input
and the learner at the same time, so a difference between two of them cannot be attributed to either.
Second, a network that receives LightGBM's prediction cannot show whether anything other than LightGBM
reaches the ceiling, since any score it obtains may be LightGBM's. Third, those models were trained under
the conference version's engagement definition and label and on inputs that leaked information from after
the cutoff (Sections~\ref{sec:definition}, \ref{sec:label} and~\ref{sec:pred-audit}). The ladder replaces
them with one question, in which the input is held fixed and only the learner changes.

\label{sec:learners-input}

% 1,015 x 7 + 1 = 7,106: AUD truth.columns_total 7106; FEAT/deep_tabular_v33_patch_full.json n_bases 1015.
Every learner predicts the \texttt{market\_event} label (Section~\ref{sec:label}) from the input of
Section~\ref{sec:pred-task}: telemetry recorded before the cutoff, 15\,s before the engagement's first
kill, summarised over an \emph{observation window} of the 30\,s that end at the cutoff, divided into six
5\,s bins. Each of 1,015 base quantities enters through seven statistics of its six bin values, and one
further column gives the age of the last participant frame, 7,106 columns in all. Engagements that the
label scores as a draw are excluded, 33,905 of 566,452, which leaves 532,547 engagements from 191,940
matches.

%   pipeline are constant across the corpus" (none of the 942 takes a second value in any of 566,452 rows).

% 0.6665 / 0.6661 / -0.0004 [-0.0010, +0.0002]: A8 learners.lgbm_paper.test_auc 0.666484, learners.lgbm_7106.
%   test_auc 0.666089; comparisons."lgbm_paper->lgbm_7106" diff -0.000394, ci95 [-0.000994, +0.000226].
% Tree cap and patience: A8 caveats[3] (lgbm_7106: 2,000-tree cap, patience 50; lgbm_paper: 400, patience 100;
%   both learning rate 0.05 and 31 leaves); colsample 0.9 in both (LGBM_PUBLISHED; run_model()).
\paragraph{Two column sets}
The tree learners and logistic regression read 6,164 columns, the neural learners all 7,106. The other
942 columns take a single value in every one of the 566,452 detected engagements and carry no information
for any learner: a tree cannot split on a constant column, and the neural pipeline, which standardises
every column with the mean and standard deviation of the training patch, turns each into zeros. The tree
pipeline removes them. Its filter reads the range of every column over all three patches, the test patch
included, but never the labels, whereas the neural pipeline's standardisation reads the training patch
alone. Of the 6,164 kept columns, 14 are constant on the labelled training engagements and 10 vary only on
the test patch; a tree fitted on the training patch cannot split on a column that is constant there, so the
test patch decides only whether such a column is carried, not what a tree learns. As a check, LightGBM
with the same learning rate, leaf limit and column fraction was fitted on both column sets. It reaches a
test AUC of 0.6665 on 6,164 columns and 0.6661 on 7,106, a paired difference of $-0.0004$ [$-0.0010$,
$+0.0002$]. The two fits also differ in their cap on trees (400 and 2,000) and in early stopping (after
100 and 50 trees without improvement), so this shows that the two column sets lead to the same result,
not that the columns alone were varied.

% Tokeniser: REPO/scripts/run_deep_tabular_baselines.py make_token_transform (reshape (rows, 7, bases) ->
%   (rows, bases, 7); unstructured columns padded [value, 0, ..., 0]); AUD checks "unstructured columns get
%   their own token, none dropped" (1015 structured tokens + 1 extra ['frame_age_s']), "every column reaches
%   exactly one token" (1016 tokens x 7), "token 0 carries base 0 under all 7 statistics".
% Intractability of one token per column: same script, docstring l.13-18 and model_deviations() "attention
%   over 1,017 tokens instead of 7,107 is the reason" (1,016 input tokens plus the summary token).
\paragraph{Tokens for the attention learners}
FT-Transformer and SAINT take their input as a set of tokens. One token per column would give 7,106
tokens, and the memory and time that self-attention needs grow with the square of the number of tokens,
which is not tractable for a corpus of this size on our hardware. Each of the 1,015 base quantities
instead becomes one token, computed by a linear map specific to that quantity from the vector of its seven
statistics. Frame age, which has no family of seven statistics, becomes a 1,016th token whose input holds
its value followed by six zeros. The input audit below confirms that every one of the 7,106 columns
reaches exactly one token. The grouping changes which relations attention models explicitly (between
quantities, not between the statistics of one quantity), not what information the model receives. The MLP
and TabNet read the 7,106 columns directly.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:learners-protocol}

% Patch rows and matches: TABF split {train 187547, val 191184, test 153816, train_matches 68116, val_matches
%   68375, test_matches 55449} (identical in FT192 and SC0 split); FEAT/model_comparison_v33_patch.json split.why
%   "a random match split lets a learner see the patch it is tested on".
\paragraph{Patch holdout}
League of Legends is updated in patches, releases that change the strength of champions and items
(Section~\ref{sec:background}). Every learner is trained on patch 15.14 (187,547 engagements from 68,116
matches), uses patch 15.15 (191,184 engagements from 68,375 matches) for early stopping and for choosing
hyper-parameters, and is scored once on patch 15.16 (153,816 engagements from 55,449 matches). A random
split of matches would put engagements of the test patch into training and let a learner use regularities
of that patch, such as which champions are strong, that are not available when predicting a later patch.
Holding out whole patches makes every result a forecast across a patch change, and it provides the
validation set that early stopping and a search need. These AUCs are not comparable with the
cross-validated AUC of Section~\ref{sec:prediction}, which mixes the three patches and trains on more
engagements (Section~\ref{sec:pred-protocol}).

\paragraph{Uncertainty and how intervals are read}
Differences between learners are paired differences in test AUC with 95\% intervals from the match
bootstrap of Section~\ref{sec:pred-protocol}~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}: in each of 2,000 resamples the
test matches are drawn with replacement and every learner is scored on the same draw. When an interval
includes zero, we write that the two learners cannot be distinguished on this test patch and do not rank
them. Intervals are per comparison; in the first comparison, Holm's adjustment of the bootstrap $p$ values
across all its comparisons changes none of the readings below. Each learner was fitted once, with one
random seed, and the resamples do not refit it, so an interval describes which matches happened to be
tested, not how much a difference would move if the learners were trained again. The window experiment
measures that second source for LightGBM on a smaller sample, where it is of the same order as the first
(Section~\ref{sec:learners-window}).

\paragraph{Two withdrawn rows}
Two rows of the first comparison are withdrawn as results. The TabNet run subtracted the entropy of its
selection masks from the loss instead of adding it, which rewards dense masks, the opposite of the method
of Arik and Pfister~\cite{arik2021tabnet}. The SAINT run formed its validation and test batches from

a common match, and 141,746 of the 153,816 test engagements shared a batch with another engagement of their
own match. Both errors are corrected in the current code, each with a test. Neither run's AUC is reported,
and neither enters any comparison in this paper except as the baseline against which its corrected run is
measured (Section~\ref{sec:learners-effort}).

\paragraph{The declared search}
A comparison in which one learner is tuned and another runs at untested settings measures tuning effort,
not model class. Every learner, LightGBM included, therefore receives a search over a grid of settings
fixed in the code before any searched model was fitted (commit \texttt{fecaaf3};
Table~\ref{tab:learner-grids}). Every grid point is fitted on training-patch engagements, stopped early on
the full validation patch and scored on the validation patch only. The point with the highest validation
AUC is selected, refitted on the full training patch with early stopping on the validation patch, and
scored on the test patch once; the code will not score the test patch again for a different selection.
The neural learners are trained with AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled}, a variant of gradient descent
that adapts the step size of each parameter and applies weight decay separately. For FT-Transformer and
SAINT the grid also chooses between a constant learning rate and a short linear warm-up followed by cosine
decay~\cite{loshchilov2017sgdr}, because a single untuned rate can leave a network under-trained or
unstable. Equal effort here means one protocol for every learner, not grids of one size: the grids hold
between 12 and 36 points.

\paragraph{Declared deviations}
The search departs from an ideal protocol and from the reference architectures in ways recorded with every
run. Grid points are fitted on the training-patch engagements of 20,000 of the 68,116 training matches; the
neural learners are trained there for at most 10 epochs, with early stopping after three, while the
selected point is refitted on the full training patch for at most 30 epochs, with early stopping after
five. The length of the warm-up-and-cosine schedule, which is set by the epoch budget, therefore differs
between selection and refit. LightGBM may grow up to 5,000 trees in both phases and stops after 50 trees
without improvement. The searched FT-Transformer and SAINT are trained with bfloat16 mixed precision, which
the first comparison and the corrected runs did not use; the MLP and TabNet are trained in full precision.
In TabNet, the two layers shared across decision steps also share their batch normalisation across steps.
SAINT's attention across engagements uses projections shared across tokens, because the dense projections
of the reference architecture would need about $4 \times 10^{9}$ parameters per stage for 1,017 tokens.
SAINT is pre-trained for three epochs, on training-patch engagements only, where Somepalli et al.\
pre-train on all unlabelled data. Every learner is fitted with one random seed.

\subsection{The Observation Window}
\label{sec:learners-window}

% 30 s / 5 s as conventions: sec_limitations.tex subsubsection "What the inputs observe" (REPO/docs/
%   DEFINITION_EVIDENCE.md section 18.1 "not derived from data, a CoG convention").
The input covers 30\,s at a resolution of 5\,s, conventions carried over from the conference version
rather than values derived from data (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). A sequence learner reading it sees
a sequence only six steps long, and a frame quantity changes at most once within it. A review attributed
the neural learners' results to this window and recommended longer windows and other resolutions. We state
that explanation as a hypothesis in two parts and test each.

\emph{Hypothesis W.} The 30\,s window with 5\,s bins limits how well engagement outcomes can be ranked on
the test patch. (W1) A longer window, or finer bins within the same window, raises test AUC. (W2) The rise
is larger for learners that read the bins in order than for LightGBM, which reads seven statistics per
quantity.

\paragraph{Design}
Window length takes the values 15, 30, 60 and 120\,s at 5\,s bins (3, 6, 12 and 24 bins), which adds
history; bin width takes the values 2.5 and 10\,s within the 30\,s window (12 and 3 bins), which changes
resolution without adding history. Every setting is built for the same 20,000 randomly sampled matches and
scored on the same 50,766 engagements from 18,335 matches, identical in rows and labels across settings,
so a difference between settings reflects the window, not a change in which engagements are scored.
LightGBM in the published configuration receives the seven statistics recomputed over each setting's bins.
It is evaluated under the patch holdout (17,909 training, 18,440 validation and 14,417 test engagements)
and, as a second protocol, under five-fold cross-validation grouped by match over all 50,766 engagements.
Each setting is compared with the 30\,s, 5\,s reference by the paired match bootstrap with 2,000
resamples, with Holm's adjustment across the five comparisons of each protocol. Because an interval of
this kind holds the fitted models fixed, the patch-holdout comparison is repeated over ten refits of every
setting that differ only in LightGBM's random seed, which selects the columns drawn for each tree; the
interval for the difference of the ten-refit means covers both the sampling of matches and the variation
between refits. With about a tenth of the full training patch, the AUCs of this sample lie below those of
Table~\ref{tab:learners}, and only differences between settings are interpreted.

\paragraph{Finer bins and longer windows for LightGBM}
Table~\ref{tab:window} gives the result. With the fitted models held fixed, only one change exceeds the
reference with an interval above zero in both protocols: 2.5\,s bins within the 30\,s window raise the

windows do not survive the adjustment in either protocol: on the patch holdout the 120\,s window's
interval, [$+0.00004$, $+0.0085$], clears zero only before adjustment (Holm-adjusted $p = 0.20$), and out

holdout comes from one seed, and it is the largest of the ten refits, which range from $-0.0007$ to

\paragraph{What this implies for Hypothesis W}
For LightGBM, W1 is not supported for window length on this sample: no longer window raises the AUC once
the five comparisons are adjusted for, in either protocol, or once refit variation is included. For bin
width, 2.5\,s bins raise the AUC of the fitted models in both protocols, but on the patch holdout the gain
cannot be distinguished from refit variation, so the evidence that finer bins help is suggestive rather
than established. For the sequence learners, the claim that the short window starves them is not supported
for window length on this sample: doubling or quadrupling the window leaves the BiGRU where it was and
lowers the Transformer, neither gains measurably more than LightGBM from the longer window (W2), and in these runs the
visible limit is the fit, since validation AUC peaks within three epochs while the training loss keeps
falling. These statements have four limits. The sample holds 20,000 matches, and its training patch about
a tenth of the engagements of the full training patch, so learners with this many parameters may behave
differently with more data. Each learner was trained with one seed and fixed, unsearched hyper-parameters.
The sequence learners were not run with finer bins, the only change whose gain for a fixed LightGBM model
survived adjustment, nor with the 15\,s window, so W2 is untested for bin width. And windows beyond 120\,s
and bins finer than 2.5\,s were not tested. A different route to temporal resolution, reconstructing each
player's state at the cutoff from the millisecond event log rather than from the last frame, is examined on
the full corpus in Section~\ref{sec:limit-evidence}.
